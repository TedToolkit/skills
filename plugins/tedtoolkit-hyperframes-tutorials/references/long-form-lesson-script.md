# Long-form spoken lesson guide

Use this guide when the user or course brief calls for a long-form, lecture-like, or chaptered
episode. Long-form does not mean stretching one explanation or preserving every outline item in the
narration. It means sustaining one useful learning journey across several connected cognitive jobs.

## Build one macro spine

Give the episode one driving question, problem, or capability. Every internal section must advance
that spine; move useful but nonessential background to the lesson card, course page, or a separate
lesson. Order sections by what the learner needs to understand next, not by the source document's
heading order.

Distinguish the primary promise from the devices used to teach it. A vivid hook, anchor case,
diagnostic routine, or analogy may occupy several minutes without becoming the episode's subject.
If the example is likely to be more memorable than the title's answer, reduce its detail, reconnect
each observation to the main claim, or choose an example whose structure directly mirrors that claim.

Begin with a brief human entry that acknowledges the learner and makes the episode feel intentionally
started, then connect it immediately to a concrete consequence, question, or result. Introduce the
instructor once when the audience has not met them; do not repeat a biography in every episode.
Adapt the entry to continuous, standalone, or independently accessible viewing instead of copying
one greeting across the course. Reveal terminology after the learner has encountered the phenomenon
when that improves understanding. Do not delay relevance or a necessary definition merely to
manufacture suspense.

## Design internal segments

Divide the lesson into a small number of conceptual sections. Each section should perform one main
cognitive job, such as predicting, observing, explaining a mechanism, comparing alternatives,
running a demonstration, or deciding what the evidence supports. Start a new section when the main
question, representation, or learner action changes.

Make boundaries audible with a short, natural signpost that says why the new section follows. End a
section with a compressed conclusion or changed understanding, not a verbatim recap. Group the
paragraph map by these sections, or add a section field to it, so later storyboarding can create
visible chapter boundaries and navigation without putting production labels into `narration.txt`.
Do not assign final timestamps before edited narration exists.

Do not force every section to the same duration and do not impose a universal maximum video length.
Instead, ensure each section has a coherent local result and reserve time for demonstrations,
prediction, inspection, and useful silence. Estimate from the complete learning actions, not from a
character or word count alone.

## Sustain attention through learning activity

Do not imitate short-form retention tactics such as repeated hooks, cliffhangers, constant novelty,
or withholding context. Reset attention by changing the learner's intellectual action: ask for a
prediction, inspect evidence, trace a process, compare two cases, explain a result, or choose a next
step. Use a new example only when it proves transfer; otherwise evolve one anchor case so the learner
does not repeatedly pay the cost of learning new context.

In-lesson questions must have an instructional purpose and a later answer, observation, or
explanation. The spoken prompt may invite prediction or retrieval, but pause duration and answer
reveal belong in the lesson card and final storyboard. Do not turn every section into a quiz or add
fake interaction that the learner cannot complete.

## Coordinate narration and visuals

Let the screen carry exact wording, notation, diagrams, tables, interface details, and dense values
while narration explains their relationship and significance. Do not read visible material line by
line unless exact wording is the object of the lesson. Avoid vague references such as "as you can
see"; name the relevant change or location so the explanation remains intelligible and accessible.

Keep terminology stable across the full episode. Introduce a term once in plain language, reuse the
same name, and distinguish deliberately between an analogy, a simplified model, and the actual
phenomenon or procedure being taught. Prefer modest conversational language that addresses the
learner directly without filler, forced intimacy, or unrelated entertaining detail.

## Consolidate and close

Use occasional synthesis to reduce memory load: state what the learner can now explain or decide,
then use that result in the next section. Synthesis should reorganize or apply prior material rather
than repeat the same definition.

Close by resolving the driving question and transferring the method to one nearby situation. Keep
course navigation outside the spoken ending for dependency-driven or branching courses. In a truly
linear course, mention a later lesson only when the course brief requests it and the preview follows
from the current unresolved problem.

## Review as a long-form episode

A long-form draft is not ready when its individual paragraphs sound natural but the whole episode
feels like adjacent mini-lessons. Review the complete arc for:

- one driving question or outcome that remains visible across sections;
- a brief, natural entry that orients the learner without repeated biography or ceremonial filler;
- title, primary outcome, dominant content, closing, and assessment all pointing to the same promise;
- section boundaries that a listener can recognize without seeing headings;
- an intentional alternation between explanation, evidence, and learner thinking;
- no long run of definitions, inventory, or course metadata;
- no unnecessary reset to a new example or vocabulary set;
- cumulative understanding, with each section using a result established earlier;
- a final synthesis that resolves the lesson rather than advertising unrelated content;
- enough production structure for later chapters, rewatching, and skimming.

## Evidence basis

These rules synthesize research rather than impose a short-video formula:

- Cynthia J. Brame, "Effective Educational Videos" (2016), DOI
  `10.1187/cbe.16-03-0125`: manage cognitive load, engagement, and active learning together.
- Philip J. Guo, Juho Kim, and Rob Rubin, "How Video Production Affects Student Engagement"
  (2014), DOI `10.1145/2556325.2566239`: MOOC engagement favors concise, personal,
  purpose-built videos, but engagement is not itself a learning measure.
- Richard E. Mayer et al., "A Personalization Effect in Multimedia Learning" (2004), DOI
  `10.1037/0022-0663.96.2.389`: modest conversational wording improved transfer in the reported
  experiments; unrelated personalization can become distracting detail.
- Karl K. Szpunar, Novall Y. Khan, and Daniel L. Schacter, "Interpolated Memory Tests Reduce Mind
  Wandering and Improve Learning of Online Lectures" (2013), DOI `10.1073/pnas.1221764110`:
  meaningful retrieval opportunities can sustain attention and learning in extended lectures.
- Niels Seidel, "Short, Long, and Segmented Learning Videos" (2024), DOI
  `10.1007/s10758-024-09745-2`: long videos can remain effective when supported by chapter
  structure, visible boundaries, navigation, and short transitions.
- Hans van der Meij and Jan van der Meij, "Eight Guidelines for the Design of Instructional Videos
  for Software Training" (2013), *Technical Communication* 60(3): design procedural video around
  supported task performance rather than spoken exposition alone.
