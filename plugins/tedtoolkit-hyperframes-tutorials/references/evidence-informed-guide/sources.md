# Sources and limits


1. Mayer et al. (2004), [A personalization effect in multimedia learning](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ684999). Three experiments on a narrated science animation found better transfer with conversational phrasing, not better retention.
2. Zhou and Lee (2025), [Teaching and learning with instructional humor](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11814177/). Structured review; learning results are mixed and many studies rely on self-report.
3. Sundararajan and Adesope (2020), [Keep it Coherent](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4). Meta-analysis of interesting but irrelevant details; the overall finding warns against decoration, not relevant levity.
4. Sailer and Homner (2020), [The Gamification of Learning](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w). Meta-analysis of actual game elements, with heterogeneous results; game fiction did not significantly change cognitive outcomes.
5. Sentance, Waite, and Kallia (2019), [Teaching computer programming with PRIMM](https://doi.org/10.1080/08993408.2019.1608781). Mixed-methods school study with a comparison group; evidence supports a structured programming-learning approach, not a specific video line.
6. Vieira, Yan, and Magana (2015), [Exploring Design Characteristics of Worked Examples](https://jocse.org/articles/6/1/1/). Introductory programming-course research; novice benefit was greater than experienced-learner benefit.
7. Roediger and Karpicke (2006), [Test-Enhanced Learning](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x/). Two experiments using prose material found better delayed retention after retrieval than restudy; transfer to other tutorial subjects must be checked.
8. Mayer and Moreno (2002), [Aids to computer-based multimedia learning](https://www.sfu.ca/~jcnesbit/EDUC220/ThinkPaper/MayerMoreno2002.pdf). Review of controlled comparisons for coordinated narration, animation, coherence, and redundancy; many examples concern science mechanisms.
9. Richter, Scheiter, and Eitel (2016), [Signaling text-picture relations](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2015.12.003). Meta-analysis; visual cues particularly helped learners with low prior knowledge.
10. Rey et al. (2019), [A Meta-analysis of the Segmenting Effect](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-018-9456-4). Meaningful segmentation improved retention and transfer on average; results varied with prior knowledge and pacing.
11. Adesope and Nesbit (2012), [Verbal redundancy in multimedia learning environments](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0026147). Meta-analysis; effects depend on prior knowledge, pacing, and whether animation or diagrams are present.
12. W3C WAI, [Description of Visual Information](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/description/). Accessibility guidance for information presented visually in synchronized media; this is a standard-setting resource, not a learning-effect experiment.
13. Mayer, Mathias, and Wetzell (2002), [Fostering understanding of multimedia messages through pre-training](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12240927/). Three experiments with mechanical-system animations found transfer benefits from teaching components and their states first. Applying this to a new topic's vocabulary is an instructional inference, not a directly tested result for every tutorial domain.
14. Tetzlaff et al. (2025), [A meta-analysis of the expertise reversal effect](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2025.102142). Sixty experiments, 5,924 learners: assistance favored low-prior-knowledge learners and less assistance favored high-prior-knowledge learners on average; effects varied by domain and learner group. Use prior knowledge, not a generic beginner label, to set guidance.
15. Renkl, Atkinson, Maier, and Staley (2002), [From Example Study to Problem Solving: Smooth Transitions Help Learning](https://www.davidlewisphd.com/courses/EDD8121/readings/2002-Renkl_et_al.pdf). Three studies of fading worked steps; evidence concerns problem-solving tasks, not a mandatory shot formula or course introduction.
16. Bisra et al. (2018), [Inducing Self-Explanation: A Meta-Analysis](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x). Meta-analysis of prompted self-explanation while studying or solving problems; it supports asking learners to articulate relations, with task and implementation differences across studies.
17. Wisniewski, Zierer, and Hattie (2020), [The Power of Feedback Revisited](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32038429/). Meta-analysis of 435 studies and over 61,000 participants; information content strongly moderates learning effects. It does not establish one universally best timing rule.
18. Höffler and Leutner (2007), [Instructional animation versus static pictures](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2007.09.013). Meta-analysis of 26 studies and 76 comparisons; the average animation advantage was larger when motion represented content rather than decorated it.
19. Berney and Bétrancourt (2016), [Does animation enhance learning?](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2016.06.005). Meta-analysis of 61 studies and 7,036 participants; the overall animation advantage was small (Hedges' g = 0.226), with important moderators. It does not warrant animating every frame.
20. Ploetzner, Berney, and Bétrancourt (2020), [A review of learning demands in instructional animations](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcal.12476). Systematic review of 194 studies; the authors found stronger support for animation when the features of change were learning relevant. This sharpens the choice between motion and stable inspection states.
21. Scherer, Siddiq, and Sánchez Viveros (2020), [A meta-analysis of teaching and learning computer programming](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2020.106349). Synthesis of 139 programming interventions; visualization conditions showed positive average effects, but those tools and learner activities differ from many narrated tutorials in other domains.
22. Fyfield, Henderson, and Phillips (2022), [Improving instructional video design: A systematic review](https://ajet.org.au/index.php/AJET/article/download/7296/1915/27086). Review of 113 papers on instructional videos; coherence, segmentation, learner control, and integrated activities had relatively strong support. Findings for verbal redundancy and modality were mixed. This review does not isolate in-frame progress indicators.
23. Rodemer et al. (2022), [Dynamic signals in instructional videos support students to navigate through complex representations](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/acp.3973). Within-subject eye-tracking study with 28 undergraduate chemistry students comparing dynamic, static, and absent cues. Its domain and sample limit direct transfer to other subjects.
24. Meier et al. (2023), [Do social cues in instructional videos affect attention allocation, perceived cognitive load, and learning outcomes under different visual complexity conditions?](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jcal.12803). Experiment with 71 adults viewing biology videos; lower visual complexity improved post-test performance, and social cues helped participants locate relevant labels in high-complexity displays. These were plant-structure videos; the same effect is not established for every domain.
25. Spanjers et al. (2012), [Explaining the segmentation effect in learning from animations: The role of pausing and temporal cueing](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0360131511003411). Randomized experiment with 161 secondary students using probability animations; pauses improved post-test achievement and temporal cues reduced mental effort.
26. W3C WAI, [Captions/Subtitles](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/captions/) and [Understanding Captions (Prerecorded)](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/captions-prerecorded.html). Accessibility guidance on selectable captions and avoiding obstruction of relevant visual content; not a learning-effect experiment.
27. W3C WAI, [Understanding Contrast (Minimum)](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html). Accessibility guidance for text and images of text on web pages; use the underlying readability concern when reviewing video frames without asserting automatic WCAG conformance for every frame.
28. Henderson and Schroeder (2021), [A systematic review of instructor presence in instructional videos](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666557321000306). Review of 12 studies found no consistent learning advantage from an on-screen instructor, while some studies reported higher learner satisfaction.
29. Fiorella and Mayer (2016), [Effects of Observing the Instructor Draw Diagrams on Learning from Multimedia Messages](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1099178). Four experiments used Doppler-effect video lessons; observing diagrams being drawn improved some outcomes, with prior knowledge and presentation conditions affecting the results. It supports considering progressive construction, not mandating drawn graphics for every topic.
30. Boucheix, Lowe, Putri, and Groff (2013), [Cueing animations: Dynamic signaling aids information extraction and comprehension](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959475212001004). An experiment on an animated piano mechanism compared cues for relations between events with entity cues and no cues. Its complex physical mechanism and learners differ from many narrated tutorial audiences.
31. Shangguan et al. (2020), [More Attractive or More Interactive? The Effects of Multi-Leveled Emotional Design on Middle School Students' Multimedia Learning](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03065/full). Two studies of middle-school learners used visual design and, in one study, playback control. Positive emotion effects were more consistent than learning or motivation effects; the science lesson and experimental designs do not prescribe an animation style for this course.
32. Hidi and Renninger (2006), [The Four-Phase Model of Interest Development](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep4102_4). Theoretical model distinguishing triggered and maintained situational interest from developing individual interest; it does not test this lesson format.
33. Chi (2009), [Active-Constructive-Interactive: A Conceptual Framework for Differentiating Learning Activities](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1756-8765.2008.01005.x). Framework and supporting studies that distinguish attending, acting, generating an explanation, and dialogue; the framework does not establish that a rhetorical question makes video watching interactive.
34. Bender, Renkl, and Endres (2026), [Punchline with(out) purpose: Integrating research on instructional humour and seductive details](https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/bjep.70080). Two narrated-slideshow experiments, one with 151 German school-age learners. Relevant humor outperformed irrelevant humor on transfer in the adult study and recall in the school study, but did not clearly outperform the control without added humor.
35. Toftness et al. (2018), [The Limited Effects of Prequestions on Learning from Authentic Lecture Videos](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jarmac.2018.06.003). Two experiments with university lecture videos found benefits concentrated on prequestioned information, with limited or marginal benefit for other information; they do not establish a question frequency for shorter videos.
36. Hefter, Kubik, and Berthold (2023), [Can prompts improve self-explaining an online video lecture? Yes, but do not disturb!](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41239-023-00383-9). Online field experiment with 124 university students in four prompt conditions; prompt types changed explanation types without significant learning-outcome differences, while self-reported interruptions correlated negatively with outcomes.
