# Visual and motion decisions

## Animation as explanation and experience

Choose motion for both comprehension and the feeling of watching the lesson. These are design
possibilities, not a recipe, effect quota, or requirement to keep every object moving:

- **Let an idea form on screen.** Drawing a path, assembling a diagram, moving an input through a
  process, or transforming one state into another can show how a result came to be. Keep identities
  recognizable when the learner needs to compare states. Experiments on instructor-drawn diagrams
  found benefits over finished diagrams in particular video lessons, especially for some learners
  with less prior knowledge; they do not require a hand-drawn style or progressive reveal everywhere
  [29].
- **Give motion a readable rhythm.** Use a spoken cue, visual change, and moment to inspect the
  result when the idea needs those phases. A pause may serve prediction, comparison, or simple
  breathing room. An experiment with secondary students found different benefits from pauses and
  temporal cues in probability animations [25]; use the actual narration and viewer experience to
  set timing rather than copying its experimental pause length.
- **Guide the eye through complex action.** A moving object, color shift, local zoom, arrow, or
  camera move can lead attention to the relevant event as it happens. A piano-mechanism study tested
  cues that connected related events rather than merely marking objects [30]. This is a reason to
  review where the eye goes, not a rule that every action needs a highlight.
- **Make room for delight.** Illustration, expressive characters, visual humor, tactile movement,
  surprising transitions, and varied color can help create interest and a recognizable voice.
  Two studies with middle-school learners found more positive emotion with attractive visual design,
  while effects on learning and motivation differed between conditions [31]. Treat enjoyment and
  understanding as separate review questions; no palette, mascot, or transition has a guaranteed
  educational effect.

Useful motion forms include progressive construction, tracking, morphing, splitting and merging,
before/after comparison, camera movement, and a brief character reaction. Pick or invent a form
that fits the subject. A technically precise process trace may need a calmer hold, while an opening or
transition may benefit from a playful flourish. Judge the continuous sequence at normal speed:
stills reveal composition, but they cannot show whether movement clarifies a relationship or holds
the viewer's interest.

## Choose what belongs in the frame

- **Give the main visual a clear purpose.** Prefer the actual demonstration, result, trace, diagram, or evolving case when it lets the learner see the claim and check a prediction. Illustration, title cards, icons, and transitions may also create curiosity, continuity, or a memorable mood; they need not each carry a fact. Judge whether the whole frame makes the current idea easy to find and whether repeated layouts make different ideas look alike. A review of 113 instructional-video papers found support for coherence and meaningful segmentation, but did not test this course's particular layout [22].
- **Reduce search before adding cues.** At each spoken cue, identify what the learner should locate and which simultaneous labels, panels, or accents make that search harder. In a biology-video experiment, lower-complexity displays led to better post-test performance, while gaze and gesture cues helped learners find relevant labels in high-complexity displays [24]. This supports removing unnecessary competitors first and then cueing the target; it does not supply a universal maximum number of labels or prove the same effect in every tutorial domain.
- **Point to the relevant feature at the relevant moment.** Tie a highlight, arrow, reveal, or local zoom to the spoken cue and the exact step, state, or comparison it concerns. Remove the cue after it has done that job; a continuously moving accent or permanently bright frame element may compete with later evidence. An eye-tracking experiment with chemistry videos found that temporally matched dynamic signals improved attention and retention and lowered extraneous load in that setting [23]. This supports targeted cueing, not a rule that all motion helps.
- **Separate instructional orientation from playback controls.** A chapter label, route map, or progress indicator belongs in the video only when it helps the learner make a meaningful course or concept decision. Specify what its units mean and keep its behavior consistent. A shot counter or bar that restarts each scene can imply lesson progress when it actually measures shot duration. The learner's pause, play, and seek controls belong to the player; a decorative in-frame imitation provides no control. The video review supports learner playback control [22], but we found no direct experiment isolating a burned-in progress bar. Treat removal of an unneeded bar as a coherence and interface inference, not an experimentally proven universal ban.
- **Check the whole viewing surface.** Inspect the video at its intended embedded size with captions enabled and native player controls available. A frame that looks clean in an isolated full-resolution screenshot may become crowded or obscure the evidence in the course page. Reserve space for optional captions without baking a duplicate transcript or seek bar into the picture. Check that the learner can still read essential steps, labels, and results against their actual backgrounds. W3C advises that captions not obstruct relevant video information and explains text-contrast requirements for web content [26, 27]. Use these as accessibility and review guidance, not as proof that one video font size improves learning or that every video pixel is covered by the same web-page criterion.
- **Treat presenter presence as a project choice.** A systematic review of 12 instructional-video studies found mixed or null learning results for showing the instructor on screen [28]. Add a face, avatar, or gesture only when it serves this lesson's social or attention goal and leaves the teaching evidence legible; do not turn a presenter overlay into a default requirement.

Design the spoken script and provisional storyboard together. For each paragraph or beat, state what the learner hears, what they see, what they should infer or do, and what evidence supports it. Remove words that merely read the screen; use visual expression to make the explanation engaging without obscuring its evidence. Keep enough context in narration to orient a listener to the frame; do not force every diagram label or exact value into the audio.

## A practical beat record

Use the lesson card's paragraph map or the storyboard itself; do not create another mandatory file. For a consequential beat, record: (1) the learner's assumed prior knowledge, (2) the one inference or action this beat serves, (3) a short spoken cue, (4) the exact visible evidence and its before/after state, (5) the feedback or check if the learner acts, and (6) where visual-only facts remain accessible. An orientation beat need not contain an exercise, and a practice beat need not introduce a new term. If a field does not apply, say why rather than manufacturing activity.

For example, a course guide can say in audio, “主线走完，你能独立完成并检验一个作品；旁边的路线可以按需要再选。” At the same moment, a persistent route map can show the exact Core and Extension names, prerequisites, and outcomes. The narration conveys the main gain and choice; a `## Visual descriptions` section in `lesson.md` gives the map's exact route information to learners through the packaged course page. A reviewer can then ask a learner to identify which route reaches a stated goal without requiring them to hear every label read aloud.
